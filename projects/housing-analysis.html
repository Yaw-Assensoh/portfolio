Why are some US cities becoming unaffordable while others remain stable?

The affordability crisis is widely reported. But is it the same everywhere? And can we prove what's driving it — or is it just housing prices doing what they always do?

LA and NY were already unaffordable before 2015 — the crisis is structural, not a recent phenomenon

The dataset starts in January 2015 with LA already at an affordability index of 1.52 — well above the 1.0 threshold. NY was at 1.14. This crisis is not a post-COVID phenomenon — it predates the dataset by years. Ten years later, LA has reached 2.22, requiring an annual household income of $200K+ to afford a median-priced home.

Median income grew $11K over 10 years while LA home values grew $400K. The regression model confirms that income increases alone have a minimal effect on affordability — a 10% income increase only moves predicted home values by +7.5%. The gap is too large for income subsidies to close. This is a supply problem, not an income problem.

The data confirms that the crisis is not uniform across the country. Houston and Chicago stayed below 1.0 for the entire 10-year period — proving that affordability is achievable in major metros. The difference is land use policy, not geography.

So what?Policymakers in high-cost cities should focus on supply-side interventions — zoning reform, density allowances, and permit streamlining — not income subsidies alone. The regression model proves that income increases have minimal effect on affordability without corresponding supply increases. Houston and Chicago provide replicable models, not geographical accidents.

Did COVID-19 change the housing market permanently — or temporarily?

Everyone knows housing prices went up during COVID. But what does the data actually show about the scale, timing, and persistence of that shift?

COVID-19 caused the sharpest simultaneous deterioration across all 5 cities on record

Between 2020 and 2022, all 5 cities deteriorated simultaneously. LA's affordability index jumped 0.302 points in 2022 alone — the largest annual shift in the entire dataset. Miami crossed the affordability threshold for the first time in 2022, driven by post-COVID migration pressure that pushed home values up 120% since 2015.

Miami's 120% home value growth is the fastest in the dataset. The city went from affordable to unaffordable in a single 2-year window. The SQL analysis using LAG window functions isolates exactly when each city's deterioration accelerated — and for all 5 cities, the inflection point is 2020-2022.

This is not a return to normal. The 2024 levels are not reverting to pre-COVID baselines. The shift is permanent in the data, and SARIMA forecasts confirm the trend continues upward through 2027.

So what?For policymakers in transitioning cities like Miami, the window for preventive action is closing. The threshold was crossed in 2022 — acting now to cap short-term rentals and incentivise long-term residential development is more urgent than waiting for a market correction that may not come. The data shows no evidence of mean reversion post-COVID.

Is affordability only possible in smaller, less desirable cities?

The narrative is that affordable cities are affordable because no one wants to live there. But Houston (2.3M) and Chicago (2.7M) disprove that. So what do they do differently?

Houston and Chicago prove affordability is achievable in major metros — and replicable

Both Houston and Chicago stayed below the 1.0 affordability threshold for the entire 10-year period — despite having populations of 2.3M and 2.7M respectively. They are not affordable because they are small or undesirable. They are affordable because of specific land use policies.

Chicago has a permissive zoning framework that allows for denser development in its core. Houston has no traditional zoning at all — a unique regulatory environment that keeps housing supply responsive to demand. Both have relatively flexible land use policies that allow the market to respond to demand more freely than coastal markets.

The regression model confirms that city location is a top predictor of home values — but Chicago and Houston show that location is not destiny. Policy choices matter more than geography. The K-Means clustering analysis placed Chicago and Houston in the same low-risk cluster, confirming their structural similarity despite different geographies and demographics.

So what?For policymakers in transitioning cities like Miami, the question is not whether affordability is possible — it is whether the policy decisions that make it possible are politically feasible. Houston's model is replicable. The question is whether there is the political will to implement it. For real estate investors, Houston and Chicago represent predictable, stable markets with narrowest confidence intervals in the SARIMA forecasts.

Where are housing prices heading over the next 3 years?

Forecasting is always uncertain. But SARIMA models trained on 10 years of monthly data can generate probabilistic forecasts with quantified uncertainty — which is more useful than a simple guess.

LA approaching $1M by December 2027 — with 95% confidence intervals that confirm the trend is real

SARIMA models were trained on 10 years of monthly data for each city, generating 36-month forecasts with 95% confidence intervals. LA is projected to approach $1M by December 2027. Miami shows the steepest projected growth at +28.4% over the next 3 years — a 120% increase since 2015, with no sign of slowing.

Chicago and Houston show the narrowest confidence intervals — the most stable and predictable markets for long-term investment. Houston is the only city where the forecast is essentially flat (-0.3% over 3 years), suggesting the market has reached a stable equilibrium. The ADF stationarity test confirmed that the data is non-stationary in levels but becomes stationary after first differencing — justifying the SARIMA approach.

The combined forecast chart shows the widening gap between LA and other cities — the most striking visual in the entire project.

So what?For homebuyers, the forecast provides a 3-year price trajectory with quantified uncertainty — not a deterministic prediction, but a probabilistic range. For real estate investors, Houston and Chicago are the most predictable markets with narrowest confidence intervals. For policymakers, the forecasts show that high-cost cities are not stabilising — they are continuing to deteriorate.

Recommendations

What should actually happen next — and for whom?

Policymakers — High-Cost Cities (LA, NY)

Reform zoning laws to allow higher residential density

The regression model shows income increases alone have minimal effect (+7.5% for a 10% income raise). The only lever that moves the affordability index meaningfully is increasing housing supply relative to demand. Fast-track construction permits, allow multi-family developments in currently zoned single-family areas, and remove parking minimums that increase construction costs.

Policymakers — Transitioning Cities (Miami)

Act now, before crossing into HIGH RISK territory permanently

Miami's trajectory — 120% home value growth since 2015 — mirrors early-stage LA and NY. The window for preventive action is closing. Cap short-term rental activity driving speculative demand, and incentivise long-term residential development before the affordability window closes completely.

Policymakers — Affordable Cities (Houston, Chicago)

Preserve the permissive zoning and land availability policies

Do not import restrictive coastal zoning frameworks. Share these land-use models with other metros through policy working groups. Houston's model works and is exportable. The data proves that affordability is achievable — the question is whether there is political will to replicate it.

Real Estate Professionals & Homebuyers

Use the 3-year SARIMA forecasts for investment planning

LA is approaching $1M by 2027. Miami has the steepest projected growth at +28.4%. Houston and Chicago show the narrowest confidence intervals — the most predictable and stable markets for long-term investment. The forecasts provide a probabilistic range, not a deterministic prediction.

Limitations & what would improve this analysis

Every honest analysis documents what it cannot do. These are the most important limitations of this project and how they would be addressed with more resources.

What this analysis cannot fully answer — and why

  • Aggregate city-level data vs individual property data. This project works with city-level averages — the highest resolution available through public sources. A true affordability analysis would require individual property transactions data (Zillow Transparent Data) to analyse neighbourhood-level variation and individual buyer characteristics. City averages can mask significant intra-city variation.
  • Forecasts are probabilistic, not predictions. SARIMA forecasts are based on historical patterns and come with 95% confidence intervals. They are not predictions of the future with certainty. Housing markets are subject to policy changes, economic shocks, and demographic shifts that are not captured in the model. Any investment decision based on these forecasts should account for this uncertainty.
  • Limited to 5 cities and 1 country. The analysis focuses on 5 major US cities. It does not cover smaller metros, non-US markets, or rural areas. A broader universe would improve the generalisability of the findings. The methodology is replicable to other markets with appropriate data sources.
  • Missing supply-side factors. The dataset includes home values, rents, and incomes — but does not include building permits, construction costs, land use regulations, or zoning density. These supply-side factors are the most important drivers of affordability but are not available at the same monthly frequency as price data. A richer dataset would enable a more complete structural model.
  • Causality vs correlation. The regression model identifies correlations between economic factors and home values. It does not prove causation. For example, the model cannot distinguish between "rent increases cause price increases" and "price increases cause rent increases" — both directions are plausible and the observational data cannot disentangle them without a natural experiment or instrumental variables approach.

Methodology

Four analytical tools were used in sequence — each answering questions the previous tool could not. The full pipeline is documented and reproducible on GitHub.

Excel

Data cleaning, VLOOKUP, PivotTables, conditional colour scales. Stakeholder-friendly dashboard with 7 sheets — clean data and analysis tabs. No technical knowledge needed to explore it.

PostgreSQL

10 business questions answered with structured queries. Window functions (RANK, LAG, AVG OVER), CTEs, CASE statements, LEFT JOIN, CREATE VIEW. All queries fully commented.

Python (7 Notebooks)

EDA, trend analysis, K-Means clustering with silhouette validation, SARIMA forecasting with ADF stationarity test, OLS regression with coefficient plot, geospatial analysis with Folium. Narrative format throughout.

Streamlit

6-page interactive dashboard with Plotly charts, Folium maps, and SARIMA forecasts. Allows users to filter by city and explore metrics on demand.
